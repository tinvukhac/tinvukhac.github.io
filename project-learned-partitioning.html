Learning-Based Spatial Partitioning

I developed a learning-based approach for choosing a spatial partitioning technique from a dataset summary, without physically partitioning each new input with every candidate method.

View the presentation

The problem

A spatial partitioner determines how data is distributed across machines and which partitions a query must examine. Grid, tree-based, and space-filling-curve methods respond differently to uniform, clustered, and skewed inputs. The best layout also depends on the objective: a range query may benefit from compact regions, while a parallel scan may care more about balanced partition sizes.

There is no universally best technique. Running all candidate partitioners over every new dataset to compare them is expensive, so the goal was to learn from prior examples and select a technique using a compact summary instead.

What I implemented

I developed a training and selection pipeline using generated datasets with diverse spatial distributions. For each training dataset, I evaluated six partitioning techniques and computed quality measures to label which technique performed best for a given objective. I compared summaries based on spatial histograms with hand-designed skewness measures, and trained neural-network models to predict a technique from those features.

The candidates in the paper include a grid, Kd-tree, STR, Z-curve, R*-Grove, and RR*-tree. The measures include total area, margin, overlap, and variation in partition cardinality; the evaluation also considers average range-query cost. These are distinct objectives, so the selected technique can change with the metric.

Training and application

  1. Generate and summarize. Create datasets with varied distributions and extract the same kind of histogram or skewness features that can later be computed for a new input.
  2. Measure candidates offline. Apply the candidate partitioners to training datasets and calculate their quality measures. These comparisons supply the labels for model training.
  3. Train and select. Train a model on summaries and labels. For a new dataset and chosen objective, compute its summary and predict a partitioning technique.
  4. Partition for execution. Pass the selected technique to the spatial processing system, which performs the actual partitioning and subsequent distributed operations. The learned selector does not itself execute queries or define their semantics.

The paper describes a Spark-based process for generating training examples. The accompanying code includes data preparation, training, and applying a selector with Beast. Candidate evaluation is expensive offline; the intended saving is avoiding that exhaustive comparison for each new dataset.

Correctness and performance tradeoffs

The central tradeoff is summary cost versus useful detail. A compact histogram or a few skewness measures are cheaper to use than physically testing every layout, but may hide features that matter to a particular partitioner. Synthetic training data can cover many patterns while still missing important real-world distributions. The model's predicted label is therefore not a guarantee of the best partitioning.

Quality metrics are also proxies for downstream behavior: minimizing overlap or area alone may not minimize the end-to-end cost of a workload. A wrong prediction should affect performance, not change query results; the chosen partitioner and downstream operators remain responsible for correct record assignment, boundary handling, and any needed duplicate avoidance.

Evaluation and what I would change

We evaluated prediction accuracy on synthetic and real datasets, comparing histogram-based and skewness-based representations as well as neural-network configurations. The paper compares selection accuracy with a random-choice baseline among six techniques; it reports separate results for synthetic and real inputs, including experiments trained on synthetic data and tested on previously unseen real datasets. Accuracy is measured against the best candidate under a selected quality metric, not as a guarantee of fastest end-to-end query time.

Given more time, I would evaluate out-of-distribution detection and confidence-aware fallbacks, measure the combined cost of summarization, partitioning, and subsequent queries, and test with evolving datasets and varied geometry sizes. I would also add differential correctness tests around partition boundaries. These are proposed extensions, not claims about the existing test suite.
